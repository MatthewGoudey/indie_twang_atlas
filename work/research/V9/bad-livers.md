Musical artist

The **Bad Livers** were an American band from [Austin, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austin,_Texas "Austin, Texas") whose inventive musical style defied attempts to categorize them according to existing genres.[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-mayhem-1)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-Labels-2)[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-3) Their influences included bluegrass, folk, punk, and other musical styles. The original lineup, formed in 1990, included [Danny Barnes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danny_Barnes_(musician) "Danny Barnes (musician)") on [banjo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banjo "Banjo"), [guitar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guitar "Guitar") and [resonator guitar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resonator_guitar "Resonator guitar"), [Mark Rubin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Rubin_(musician) "Mark Rubin (musician)") on [upright bass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upright_bass "Upright bass") and [tuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuba "Tuba"), and [Ralph White III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_White "Ralph White") on [fiddle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiddle "Fiddle") and [accordion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accordion "Accordion"). Barnes composed the majority of the group's original songs.[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-BNL-4) When White left the group at the end of 1996, he was briefly replaced by Bob Grant on [mandolin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandolin "Mandolin") and guitar. Barnes and Rubin then continued to perform and record as a duo until unofficially dissolving the band in 2000. The band has neither toured nor recorded since then, but Barnes and Rubin have played a few live shows with Grant in 2008, 2009, and 2014.[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-5)[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-6)

The Bad Livers' music has often been cited for its influence on other groups,[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-Phantom-7)[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-8)[\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-9)[\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-10) creating what _[The Austin Chronicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Austin_Chronicle "The Austin Chronicle")_ described as "an impressive legacy".[\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-11) _[The Stranger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Stranger_(newspaper) "The Stranger (newspaper)")_ credited them with "revitalizing roots music",[\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-12) and, according to the _[San Francisco Chronicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Chronicle "San Francisco Chronicle")_, "The Bad Livers helped open the way for old-time and bluegrass bands of today".[\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-13)

## History

The Bad Livers formed in 1990, when Barnes "came up with this idea that it would be great to have a small-format acoustic band that could play different kinds of music \[at a\] virtuoso level, where they could play any kind of music".[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-Horizons-14) He began playing with Rubin and White,[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-Freedom-15) and the trio adopted the name Bad Livers in the summer of 1990.[\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-16) They began playing frequently in Austin,[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-mayhem-1) including a weekly set at the Saxon Pub.[\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-17) A typical set in 1991[\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-18)[\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-19) included a wide variety of styles and periods of music, as Rubin later explained: "We were doing [Mississippi John Hurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippi_John_Hurt "Mississippi John Hurt"), [gospel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_music "Gospel music") tunes, [Captain Beefheart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captain_Beefheart "Captain Beefheart")—anything, really, but it was [Motörhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mot%C3%B6rhead "Motörhead") or the [Misfits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misfits_(band) "Misfits (band)") that caught on."[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-The_Horror-20)

Imprecise descriptions of the Bad Livers' music abounded: "thrash-grass",[\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-21) "acoustic bluegrass with a punk death wish",[\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-Houston-22) "something called 'contemporary bluegrass,'"[\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-23) and "acoustic-metal-bluegrass",[\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-Colorfast-24) although these same sources would cite the band as "much-lauded"[\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-Colorfast-24) and note that "the musicians can really play".[\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-Houston-22) The recurrence of the term [bluegrass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluegrass_music "Bluegrass music"), however bizarrely it was modified, along with counter-assessments of them as "good, faithful traditionalists" who "preserve the spirit as well as the technique of old-timey folk music",[\[25\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-25) created a generic perplexity that led Austin country legend [Don Walser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Walser "Don Walser") to observe that the only question he couldn't answer was what kind of music the Bad Livers played.[\[26\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-26)

Barnes insisted that the Bad Livers were not a bluegrass band,[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-Labels-2)[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-BNL-4) but had created an original sound: "This isn't bluegrass and it isn't this or that. It's Bad Liver music. We end up making our own thing."[\[27\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-27) Barnes' original compositions were featured on their first album, _Delusions of Banjer_,[\[28\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-28) released in 1992 on [Quarterstick Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarterstick_Records "Quarterstick Records") and produced by [Paul Leary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Leary "Paul Leary") of the [Butthole Surfers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butthole_Surfers "Butthole Surfers"). The album was praised for "Barnes's strong material, as well as the group's tight musical interaction".[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-BNL-4) Barnes credited the latter with improving the former: "The musical telepathy is really good. I can sort of tailor-make a song to the guys' playing, make the song fit what they're doing, since I write most of the material."[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-BNL-4)

Rubin claimed that they had decided to incorporate the accordion and tuba into the band to counteract their growing popularity,[\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-29) but to no avail: their audience continued to widen and more critics saw beyond the gimmicky descriptions to the band's innovation and skill. _[The Washington Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Washington_Post "The Washington Post")_ described them as "truly great",[\[30\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-30) _[The Times-Picayune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times-Picayune "The Times-Picayune")_ praised their "serious musicianship" and Barnes' "soulful, urgent lead vocals",[\[31\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-31) while _[Rolling Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolling_Stone "Rolling Stone")_ admired their "striking blend of virtuoso flash and poignant simplicity".[\[32\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-32) Don McLeese of the _[Austin American-Statesman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austin_American-Statesman "Austin American-Statesman")_ twice described the Bad Livers as "Austin's best band"[\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-33)[\[34\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-34) and raved of one live show: "The uncommon telepathy enjoyed by Danny Barnes, Mark Rubin and Ralph White makes the band's frenetic acoustic interplay sound like the work of a six-armed, multistringed monster."[\[35\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-35) Barnes was voted "Best Player of Any Other Instrument" for the banjo at the Austin Music Awards in 1991 and 1992,[\[36\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-36) and the Bad Livers were voted "Best None of the Above" for four consecutive years from 1991 to 1994.[\[37\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-37)

The Bad Livers' second album, _[Horses in the Mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horses_in_the_Mines "Horses in the Mines")_, was produced by Barnes and released on Quarterstick in 1994.[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-Freedom-15) In his review, McLeese admired them as "a band drawing from the wellspring of tradition to create something fresh, vital and original".[\[38\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-38) _[Süddeutsche Zeitung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%BCddeutsche_Zeitung "Süddeutsche Zeitung")_ found that the record "sounded quite authentic—almost as if it had long held a place in the Library of Congress".[\[39\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-39) Raoul Hernández poetically concurred, saying that the record sounded "like a bunch of skeletons cackling into the sole microphone that dangles from the outhouse ceiling".[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-Freedom-15)

The Bad Livers spent 1995 and 1996 touring and working on side projects[\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-The_Horror-20) as well as looking for a new label to release their next album.[\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-40) Finally the band signed a three-album deal with [Sugar Hill Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_Hill_Records_(bluegrass_label) "Sugar Hill Records (bluegrass label)"), which had more experience than Quarterstick at "selling banjo records".[\[41\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-41) Their first album for the label, _[Hogs on the Highway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hogs_on_the_Highway "Hogs on the Highway")_, was released along with the information that White had decided to leave the band.[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-Labels-2)[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-Phantom-7) It was announced that he was to be replaced by Bob Grant, though Grant's tenure with the band did not last long.[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-Freedom-15)[\[42\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-Rubin-42) Both White and Grant appeared on _Hogs on the Highway_, which received enthusiastic reviews from _[Sing Out!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sing_Out! "Sing Out!")_, praising Barnes' "quirky and inventive" original songs,[\[43\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-43) and the _Old-Time Herald_, admiring the "considerable skill" with which Barnes crafted his lyrics.[\[44\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-44) The _Austin American-Statesman_ agreed that Barnes was "an entirely underrated songwriter" as well as a "banjo wizard",[\[45\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-45) while _The Washington Post_ lauded his "timeless, deadpan voice".[\[46\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-46) _[The Sydney Morning Herald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sydney_Morning_Herald "The Sydney Morning Herald")_ was appreciative of the "beautifully controlled ambience"[\[47\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-47) of the entire album.

Shortly after the album's release, Barnes moved from Austin to [Port Hadlock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Hadlock "Port Hadlock"), [Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_(state) "Washington (state)"),[\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-Horizons-14)[\[48\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-48) though he and Rubin continued to work closely together on the score for the [Richard Linklater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Linklater "Richard Linklater") film _[The Newton Boys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Newton_Boys "The Newton Boys")_ as well as the Bad Livers' next album. Although the soundtrack was admired by _[The Village Voice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Village_Voice "The Village Voice")_[\[49\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-49) and _[Entertainment Weekly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entertainment_Weekly "Entertainment Weekly")_[\[50\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-50) as well as the Austin papers,[\[51\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-51)[\[52\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-52) the film did not fare well at the box office,[\[53\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-53)[\[54\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-54) and the soundtrack album did not sell well.[\[42\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-Rubin-42) The Bad Livers' second album for Sugar Hill, _Industry and Thrift_, was released in September 1998 with only Barnes and Rubin credited as members of the band,[\[55\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-IAT-55) though the album features various guest musicians, including members of Rubin's side project, Rubinchik's Orkestyr,[\[56\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-56) who are featured on the track "A Yid Ist Geboren inz Oklahoma".[\[55\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-IAT-55) The album garnered positive reviews,[\[57\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-57)[\[58\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-58)[\[59\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-59) and the _[Chicago Tribune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Tribune "Chicago Tribune")_ selected it as the best bluegrass album of 1998,[\[60\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-60) while _[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times "The Times")_ of London admired its "genuine originality".[\[61\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-61) _Industry and Thrift_ did not receive as much attention from the press as _Hogs on the Highway_ had, however, and as Barnes lamented, the album "fell off the face of the earth".[\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-Freedom-15)

The Bad Livers' final album, _Blood and Mood_, was released in February 2000 and featured, as _The Austin Chronicle_ noted with astonishment: " _Electric_ punk rock, sample-based tunes with drum tracks, and a shocking scarcity of juiced-up banjo playing".[\[62\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-62) _[The Independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Independent "The Independent")_ named it "the leftfield album of the week" and found that it "brims with enthusiastic ideas",[\[63\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-63) while _The Washington Post_ was puzzled but invigorated by a live show supporting the album, concluding that it had been "Fascinating. Even refreshing".[\[64\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-64) Although it left _[No Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_Depression_(magazine) "No Depression (magazine)")_ depressed,[\[65\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-65) Daniel Wolff found the album to be uniquely evocative of a "rural eccentricity"[\[66\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-Wolff-66) that had not yet been extinguished and formed a kind of continuum with punk and old-time country: "Either the Bad Livers pick up where 'I Wish I Were a Mole in the Ground' left off, or [Bascom Lunsford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bascom_Lunsford "Bascom Lunsford") discovered the punk aesthetic in the 1920s."[\[66\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-Wolff-66) _Blood and Mood_ did not sell well,[\[67\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-blerg-67) and by the summer of 2000 the Bad Livers appeared to be "over for good".[\[67\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-blerg-67)

The Bad Livers were inducted into the Austin Music Hall of Fame in 2007.[\[68\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Livers#cite_note-68)
